# Lecturer's Notes: Perception Workshop

[Student workshop](./session01_03_04.md) · [Resource guide](./session01_03.md)

## Purpose

Use the four existing activities as the structure for the perception and practice block. Students first encounter a question or experience, then consult a resource, then reconsider their answer. The 70-minute proposal applies to this block only; it does not include the earlier AI-learning and human-learning parts of Session 01.

Look for students who can make a useful distinction, support it with an observation or source, and explain what remains uncertain. Agreement about whether AI “really perceives” is not required.

## Prepare

- Open the dedicated [Maurice vs George board][miro-perception]. Make space for each group's initial idea, observation or source, revised idea, and open question. Paper notes are a fallback.
- Have a few safe objects and an opaque bag ready. For navigation, check a clear, level route and keep participation voluntary. Offer the touch or design alternative without requiring an explanation.
- Make [Berkeley's section 6][berkeley-section-6] available on screen or as a short handout. This is the default verification route because it is brief and available in English.
- If using Merleau-Ponty for source verification, prepare the specified [French Preface paragraph][merleau-ponty-preface] or a corresponding English excerpt with edition and page. Otherwise use the short course introduction in Activity 3 and Berkeley for Activity 4.
- If using the NotebookLM podcast, preview it and select a 2–3-minute passage about the relevant idea before class. Use it **within** the resource time instead of adding a full listening task. The short written introductions are ready-to-use alternatives.
- Check access to the notebook, board, and an LLM. A shared model response can replace individual accounts; a saved response can support the source-checking activity if live access fails.

## Suggested Facilitation

| Minutes | What to do | What to listen for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–10 | Let pairs classify examples; surface one disagreement | Different meanings of “perception,” rather than correct categories |
| 10–25 | Run the sensors scenario; introduce Berkeley after the first answers | Distinctions between the room, stored map, input, and possible experience |
| 25–40 | Run one embodied activity; introduce Merleau-Ponty afterward | A concrete observation separated from an interpretation |
| 40–60 | Generate a short LLM explanation; check one claim against a passage | A specific source-based correction or a clearly stated limit |
| 60–70 | Revisit opening positions and connect one distinction to design | A justified revision or defense, plus an open question |

Do not require every group to present after every activity. Select one disagreement or surprising example and let others respond. Give students a moment to think individually before asking for consensus.

## Conceptual Guardrails

**Berkeley:** avoid “the world disappears when nobody looks.” His account includes an eternal spirit. The robot is our analogy; a map stored in memory is not automatically a mind or an act of perception.

**Merleau-Ponty:** bodily experience is more than adding sensors. Ask how action and situation shape what appears. A brief eyes-closed exercise is not a simulation of living with blindness.

**AI:** distinguish different systems and architectures. A stationary classifier, interactive robot, and text-only chatbot do not all have the same access to the world. Separate observable performance from claims about subjective experience; this workshop cannot establish or rule out the latter.

**Sources:** an LLM speaking “as Berkeley” produces an interpretation. Have students inspect an underlying passage and distinguish historical claims from speculative applications. NotebookLM is useful for navigation and explanation, but a generated answer is not independent confirmation of another generated answer.

## If Discussion Needs More Time

Treat 70 minutes as a proposal to test. A shorter, 45-minute version can use Activities 1–3 (8, 12, and 15 minutes), followed by 10 minutes of reflection. Move Activity 4 to a later class with its own source-checking time. Avoid compressing verification into a quick vote on which response sounds convincing.

Students do not need to finish the full recordings or reading list. Invite them to choose a resource afterward only if it helps pursue a question or project idea.

## Review After Teaching

Keep a few examples of initial and revised positions, including disagreement. Ask:

- Did the resource change the argument or only add a philosopher's name?
- Could students point to evidence for their claim?
- Did the workshop produce a useful creative question?
- Which activity needed more space, and which resource was actually helpful?

Use those observations to decide whether to extend this format to the human-learning material.
