## Case file: a literary controversy

In September 2026, **Thélyson Orélien's** novel *C'était ça ou mourir* was receiving attention and literary acclaim in France. Claims then circulated that the book had been produced using generative AI. **Orélien disputed the accusation**, and the question of authorship became a public controversy.

The case raises issues involving the **writer**, **publisher**, **readers**, **literary critics and prizes**, and other creators. An allegation, or a score produced by an AI-text detector, is **not itself proof of how the book was made**. We will examine the controversy's consequences and the questions it raises, **not determine whether the allegation is true**.

**Two questions for your pair:**
- If you enjoyed the book before hearing the allegation, might you now judge it differently?
- What information about the creative process would you want, and why?

## Used cars and trust: the "lemons" analogy

This example, from economist **George Akerlof**, explains what happens when one person knows something important that another person cannot check.

1. **Used cars:** Two cars look similar, but one has hidden problems (a "lemon"). The **seller knows** more about the car than the **buyer**.
2. **Trust changes:** Because the buyer can't identify the reliable car, they might offer a low price for **both**. Sellers of good cars may refuse the low offer. Suspicion can therefore harm honest sellers too.
3. **Now books:** Writers and publishers often know more than readers about how a book was produced. Some readers value the text alone, while others also care about human effort, intention, and disclosure. If the process is unclear, suspicion about AI involvement could affect **all** authors, including those who did not use AI.

The economic concept is **asymmetric information**: one side knows more than the other.

**Where the analogy stops:** AI-assisted writing is **not** automatically a defective product. A book's artistic quality is not the same as a car's mechanical condition. The comparison is about **uncertainty, trust, and what people value**, not about deciding which writing is better.

## Stakeholders: six questions, one controversy

**One book, different perspectives:** writer → publisher → readers, critics, and other creators. Each may value different things about the same work.

Different people can evaluate the same novel in different ways.

| Stakeholder | What might matter to them? |
| --- | --- |
| **Writer** | Recognition for their choices and work; reputation; being judged fairly |
| **Reader** | The story and experience; trust in information about authorship |
| **Publisher** | Editorial responsibility; public trust; sales and reputation |
| **Critic or prize jury** | How to evaluate the work; transparent criteria; evidence |
| **Other writers and creatives** | Future opportunities; standards for credit and fair competition |
| **AI company** | Use of its system; accountability; commercial and social effects |

Use these six lenses when comparing **two** stakeholders:

| Lens | Ask |
| --- | --- |
| **Agency** | Who makes decisions, and who has influence? |
| **Creativity** | Who contributes ideas, experience, and intention? |
| **Value** | Who gets credit, money, or recognition? |
| **Risk** | Who might lose trust, opportunities, or reputation? |
| **Ownership** | Who can claim or control the work? What remains uncertain? |
| **Success** | What would count as success for each person? |

**Your group's goal:** Find one genuine disagreement between two perspectives. You do not need to decide whether AI use is good or bad.

## Connecting learning, perception, and creativity

Remember the earlier parts of this class:

- **Pépin:** Learning and creation are connected to failure, relationships, culture, and freedom.
- **Merleau-Ponty:** We discover and understand through bodily engagement, movement, and action, not only through abstract plans.
- **The black box:** An output alone does not always tell us how it was produced.

**Discussion question:** Is the value of a creative work only in the finished object, or also in the human experience, intention, effort, and relationships behind it?

## Further exploration (optional)

These are possible **questions to investigate**, not quotations verified from the podcast:

- **Automatisation du symbolique:** What happens to shared meaning when generating words and images becomes automated?
- **Imperfection:** Can struggle, hesitation, and failure matter artistically, beyond a polished result?
- **Universel abstrait:** Does familiar, generalised expression risk losing individual context?
- **Écoute, patience, effort:** What do we learn from listening to other people, practising, and working through resistance?
- **Faire monde:** Can creating be a way of belonging to and changing a shared world?
- **Passenger seat:** When do AI tools support creative choice, and when do they leave us simply accepting existing options?
